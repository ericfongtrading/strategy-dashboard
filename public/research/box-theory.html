Where the idea came from

Start with the one index nobody beats

Most active fund managers fail to beat the S&P 500 over time. That is strange — thousands of smart, well-resourced people, losing to a list. So the question that started this project was simple: what does the index do that they don't?

The answer isn't magic, it's a filter. To stay in the index a company has to keep earning its place among the largest by market value. Companies that stall drop out; companies that compound rise in. The index quietly, mechanically lets its winners run and cuts its losers — with no ego, no story, no fear of missing out. That is the behaviour managers struggle to follow and the index does for free.

Box Theory asks whether that same filter can be written as an explicit, testable rule: buy leaders exactly as they break out of a consolidation, ride them while they work, and cut them fast when they don't — and whether it generalises beyond the US.

There is one trap on this path, and it is the reason most of the work here went into method rather than ideas. The instant you study "the winners," you invite survivorship bias — testing a rule only on the companies that happened to survive is the easiest way to fake a brilliant backtest. Eliminating it, carefully and provably, is what the rest of this page is about.

The rule

What a "box" is, and how a trade plays out

After a move, a stock pauses and coils into a tight range — the box. Supply and demand balance, volume dries up. The break above the box is the market voting that the pause is over.

Anatomy of a Box Theory trade

Illustrative schematic of the mechanic — entry, risk, and exit.
Entry
Buy the break above the box ceiling, confirmed by volume expanding as price clears the range.
Risk
Initial stop sits just under the box floor. The box defines the risk before the trade is taken — that fixed risk is one "R".
Ride
As price advances, the stop trails up beneath it, locking in progress while leaving room for the trend.
Exit
Out when the trend breaks and the trailing stop is hit. Many trades stop out near breakeven; a few run for many R.

Accumulation — pressing the winners

Size is added on an R-ladder, measured in the trade's own risk rather than in new chart patterns. Every time the position gains another fixed step of open profit, one more equal-risk tranche goes on — in most markets a rung at every +½R up to +5R, ten in all; in a few, coarser steps and a lower ceiling, fit per market like every other parameter. Adds only fire while volatility is still expanding, and the whole stack sits behind a single stop that ratchets up and never down.

Why it helps
The rare trends that run for months are where the money is. Adding to them concentrates capital in exactly those names — this is a big part of why the biggest winners reach +9R, +13R. Re-running the held-out book with the adds switched off cuts average R per trade by half to two-thirds, so the accumulation isn't a garnish on the edge; it is a large share of it.
Only into expansion
A rung fills only on a bar where the 20-day Bollinger band is wider than its own 20-day average — volatility opening up, the trend accelerating. Price can drift up through a rung in a quiet tape and no add is taken. Pressing is reserved for trends that are actually moving.
Risk stays bounded
Every tranche sits behind one shared stop that only moves up: to breakeven once the trade is 1R ahead, then onto a volatility (ATR) trail, and tighter still — under the recent 10-day low — as soon as more than one tranche is on. The adds are bought with open profit, and the whole stack leaves together when the stop goes.

Every trade carries a stop — and gaps are modelled honestly

A stop sits under the box from the moment the trade opens, and the position is sized so a normal stop-out costs exactly one R — that is the definition of 1R. Most of the losers in the trade list sit right around −1R.

A few losses are bigger — the −1.6R on AMT, for instance. That's a gap down: news hits overnight and the stock opens below the stop, so the exit fills at the worse opening price rather than the stop price. The backtest fills at that real gapped price instead of pretending the stop always holds — which is why you occasionally see a loss past −1R. Gaps cut both ways and sometimes jump a winner further in your favour; modelling them is the conservative, honest choice.

Data & method

How the numbers were made — and kept honest

Everything rests on the data handling. Here is exactly what was done, and why each choice closes a way a backtest can lie.

Source. End-of-day price and volume for every market, from EODHD — roughly 14 GB spanning 2011 to today. Critically, it includes delisted and dead tickers, not just today's survivors.

Top-market-cap basket, rotated through time. Mirroring how an index works, each market is reduced to its ~500 most liquid names — a proxy for the investable leaders — rebuilt as time passes so the basket rotates: names enter as they grow into it and leave as they fade. A company that was tradeable in 2016 and died in 2019 is in the test in 2016 and gone by 2019, exactly as it happened. No hindsight about who survived.

Costs and liquidity. Commissions and slippage are inside every trade's net result. Trades in names below ~$100k median daily turnover are dropped, so no part of the edge can hide in untradeable micro-caps.

Held-out walk-forward. Parameters are fit only on past data (2011 → an anchor year), then scored once on the untouched window after it. This is repeated at four anchors — 2015, 2018, 2021, 2024 — and the forward slices are stitched into one continuous out-of-sample record from 2015 to 2026. The same rules, re-fit at each anchor, stay profitable across all of them: that repetition is the consistency check.

The one that matters most

Survivorship bias — and why we hunt it down

This is the single most common way a backtest flatters itself, and the reason this took weeks of data work rather than days.

What it is. Survivorship bias is judging something by the survivors alone, while the ones that dropped out stay invisible. Test a rule only on the stocks that still trade today and you have quietly stacked the deck: today's listings are disproportionately the companies that kept winning. The ones that broke out, failed and were delisted — bankruptcies, busted mergers, frauds — aren't in the data at all.

Why it's poison for a breakout rule. Box Theory buys strength — stocks making new highs out of a base. A painful share of spectacular blow-ups made new highs right before they collapsed. Bear Stearns had real, tradeable breakouts in its good years, then went to zero. A survivors-only database holds neither: it deletes Bear Stearns entirely, erasing its winning trades and, more importantly, its catastrophic ending. Keep only survivors and you systematically strip out losing trades the rule would really have taken — so it looks far better than it ever was.

Why including delisted names is the right call. Your intuition is exactly right: a company gone today was alive and tradeable fifteen years ago, and the rule should be judged on how it traded then. So we keep every delisted and dead ticker and rebuild the tradeable universe as of each date — a liquid leader in 2016 is in the test in 2016, and gone the year it actually died. Nothing is chosen with hindsight. That is the honest population: winners that kept winning, winners that later died, and everything between. Less flattering, and the only version worth trusting.

Honest caveats

What this is, and isn't

  • Equal-risk comparison. The strategy is scaled to the S&P's ~12% volatility so the two take the same risk; Sharpe and Calmar don't depend on that scaling.
  • Monthly basis. Both sides use monthly data; the S&P series is a monthly index level that understates sharp intra-month crashes — a conservative choice for the comparison.
  • Price-based, both sides. Neither side includes dividends, so the comparison is like-for-like.
  • Sizing not yet optimised. Results are in R; position sizing and portfolio allocation are the next phase, not claimed here.
  • It's a wide book. The edge lives in breadth — many small positions at once — so it's built for automated, diversified execution, not a handful of hand-picked trades.
  • Down years are real. 2018 and 2022 lost money; a long-only breakout book should.
  • Access varies. Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam carry real edge but need a local/prop route or have limited emerging-market access. Hong Kong and the United States are fully retail-tradeable (Interactive Brokers / Futu); Hong Kong sits outside the conservative core only because its edge is more recent and concentrated, not for access reasons.
